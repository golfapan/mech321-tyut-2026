Why study system dynamics? · Start with something you can see

One pendulum. Two equilibria. What changes?

These are two resting positions of the same rigid-rod pendulum. The mass, length, gravity and pivot damping are identical, and no external torque is applied. Both satisfy the same equation of motion. Why does a small nudge have such different consequences?

Solid rod and filled bob: equilibrium. Dashed rod and open bob: a small displacement to the right. Yellow arrow: gravity. The sketches use a small angle ε>0. Throughout this guide, θ is measured from the downward vertical.

One equation of motion: Iθ̈+bθ̇+mgℓ sinθ=0
Two equilibrium solutions: θ(t)≡0 and θ(t)≡π, with zero angular velocity and acceleration.
Think first: If a stationary position satisfies the equation, does that guarantee it can withstand a small perturbation? What else must we find out?
From these pictures to the purpose of this course

For free motion, set θ̇=θ̈=0 in the equation. This gives sinθ=0: the downward and upright equilibria, treating angles differing by 2π as the same position. The constant functions θ(t)≡0 and θ(t)≡π are equilibrium solutions. Oscillations and rotations are other motions described by the same equation.

Equilibrium asks whether a system can remain at rest. Stability asks what happens after a small departure. Substitution answers the first question. To answer the second, we will model the system, linearise it and analyse its poles. Energy supplies the additional argument needed in the undamped case.

Use equations to understand behaviour

Start with an observation. Use the modelling ideas from Lecture 1 to express the physical relationships. Then ask: will a perturbation decay or grow? Will the system oscillate? How quickly will it settle? How could a design change improve its response?

The mathematics serves these questions. Understand inertia, damping and restoring action, choose an appropriate analysis method, and check its predictions against an experiment or simulation.

STEP 01 · Define the physical system first

From “gravity pulls it back” to a torque balance

Model a point mass m on a light, rigid rod of length ℓ, rotating in a vertical plane about a fixed pivot. Neglect the rod's mass and air resistance. Approximate pivot friction by viscous damping proportional to angular velocity.

Measure θ from the downward vertical, positive towards the right in the sketch. Thus θ=0 is downward and θ=π is upright. Use radians for angles and seconds for time. A pivot actuator may apply torque u(t), positive in the same rotational direction. For free release, u=0.

Predict first: Pull the pendulum slightly to the right and release it from rest. Which way is its initial angular acceleration? Without friction, would its energy disappear?
Moment of inertia: I=mℓ2
Gravitational torque: −mgℓ sinθ; damping torque: −bθ̇
Iθ̈ = u − bθ̇ − mgℓ sinθ
Hence: Iθ̈+bθ̇+mgℓ sinθ=u(t)

Here m is mass (kg), ℓ is length (m), g is gravitational acceleration (m/s2), I is the moment of inertia about the pivot (kg·m2), b≥0 is the rotational damping coefficient (N·m·s/rad), and u is applied torque (N·m). Angular velocity θ̇ is in rad/s and angular acceleration θ̈ in rad/s2. A dot denotes differentiation with respect to continuous time.

Which parts are physical laws, and which are assumptions?

Begin with rotational dynamics, Στ=Iθ̈. The fixed pivot, massless rigid rod and point mass are idealisations. The term −bθ̇ is a friction model; −mgℓ sinθ follows from the gravitational moment arm. The rod force acts through the pivot, so its moment about the pivot is zero.

The input is u(t); one possible output is θ(t). Mass, length and damping are parameters. Initial angle and angular velocity are initial conditions. Changing the input and changing the release conditions are different experiments. Dry friction, a heavy rod or flexibility would require revised assumptions.

Why do we need both angle and angular velocity?

At the lowest point, the pendulum may be travelling left or right. The same angle with a different angular velocity gives a different future motion. Choose x1=θ and x2=ω=θ̇, and specify θ(0) and ω(0). With the input known, these two state variables are sufficient to start a prediction.

θ̇=ω
ω̇=−(b/I)ω−(g/ℓ)sinθ+u/I
Predict first: Two trials start at θ=0, one with ω=+1 rad/s and the other with ω=−1 rad/s. Will their next moments be the same?

Two coupled first-order state equations still describe a second-order system. Two independent initial values are still needed. Rewriting has not reduced the order. The sinθ term makes the model nonlinear.

STEP 02 · Linearise about each equilibrium

The difference lies in the sign of cosθ*

For free motion, u=0. Let θ* be an equilibrium angle and define the small deviation δ(t)=θ(t)−θ*. Since θ* is constant, θ̇=δ̇ and θ̈=δ̈. All angles below are in radians.

sin(θ*+δ) ≈ sinθ*+cosθ*·δ
At equilibrium, sinθ*=0, so sinθ≈cosθ*·δ.
Iδ̈+bδ̇+mgℓ cosθ*·δ=0

Retain terms linear in δ and neglect higher-order terms. This describes small motions near the chosen equilibrium. Both resting positions satisfy the original equation, but their local models differ:

Downward: θ*=0

cosθ*=+1

Iδ̈+bδ̇+mgℓδ=0

Local effective stiffness: keff=+mgℓ. A positive deviation produces a negative gravitational torque: a restoring action.

Upright: θ*=π

cosθ*=−1

Iδ̈+bδ̇−mgℓδ=0

Local effective stiffness: keff=−mgℓ. A positive deviation produces a positive gravitational torque, increasing the departure.

Make a prediction: Keep inertia and damping unchanged, but reverse the sign of the restoring term. How should the motion change? Characteristic roots will make the answer precise.
How small must the motion be?

The deviation δ must be sufficiently small. Near the downward equilibrium, sinθ differs from θ by about 0.5% at 10°, but about 17% at 60%, relative to θ. These figures describe the approximation of the gravity term, not a guaranteed error bound for a trajectory. Use the same definition δ=θ−θ* at both equilibria.

STEP 03 · The key analysis: decay or growth?

Both are equilibria. Why is only one stable?

Substitute an exponential mode δ(t)=Cest into the linearised equation. C is the modal coefficient; s is a characteristic root, with units of s−1. For a nonzero mode, cancel the common factor:

Is2+bs+mgℓ cosθ*=0
s2+(b/I)s+(g/ℓ)cosθ*=0　(I=mℓ2)

These roots determine the exponential modes of the natural response. For this model, they are also the poles of the linearised torque-to-angle transfer function. The real part determines decay or growth; the imaginary part determines oscillation. Assume I>0, g>0, ℓ>0 and b≥0.

Downward: positive constant term

s2+(b/I)s+g/ℓ=0
s=−b/(2I) ± √[(b/(2I))2−g/ℓ]

For b>0, both roots have negative real parts. A complex pair has real part −b/(2I). For real roots, the square root is smaller than b/(2I), so even the “plus” root is negative. Equivalently, real roots with a negative sum and positive product must both be negative.

All modes decay. The linearised system returns asymptotically to equilibrium, and the original nonlinear downward equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable.

For b=0, s=±j√(g/ℓ), where j2=−1. The linear model oscillates at constant amplitude. It does not settle. Stability of the original nonlinear pendulum also needs the energy argument below.

Upright: negative constant term

s2+(b/I)s−g/ℓ=0
s=−b/(2I) ± √[(b/(2I))2+g/ℓ]

The square root always exceeds b/(2I), so there is one positive real root and one negative real root. Equivalently, the product of the two real roots is −g/ℓ<0: they have opposite signs.

The positive root s+ produces a growing mode es₊t. A general small perturbation grows, making the upright equilibrium unstable. In the angle–angular-velocity phase plane, it is a saddle point.

Increasing b makes the positive root smaller and the negative root more negative. The pair does not simply shift left. For every finite b≥0, the positive root remains positive: passive damping alone cannot stabilise the upright pendulum.

Return to the opening question: Both resting positions have zero velocity and acceleration. Their different stability comes from the sign of the local restoring term and the resulting pole locations.
Why do imaginary-axis poles need an energy argument?

If all linearised roots have negative real parts, the nonlinear equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable. A root with positive real part establishes instability. With zero real parts, linearisation alone is generally inconclusive about the nonlinear system.

E=½Iδ̇2+mgℓ(1−cosδ)
For free motion: Ė=−bδ̇2

Near the downward equilibrium, δ=θ. When b=0, energy is conserved, and δ=δ̇=0 is a strict local energy minimum. Sufficiently small initial energy keeps the state nearby, establishing stability. A nonzero small energy cannot disappear, so the equilibrium is not asymptotically stable. The linear model's constant-amplitude oscillation is consistent with this conclusion.

The upright model has a negative root too. Why is it unstable?

The linearised motion is δ(t)=C+es₊t+C−es₋t, where s+>0 and s−<0. Initial angle and angular velocity determine C+ and C−. Exactly satisfying δ̇(0)=s−δ(0) makes C+=0, leaving only the decaying mode. A general small error excites the growing mode.

Stability requires every sufficiently small perturbation to remain nearby. A few specially chosen trajectories approaching equilibrium are not enough. The straight-line condition above belongs to the linearised model; it is not an exact initial-state condition for the nonlinear pendulum.

The poles now explain a physical response: real parts describe decay or growth, and imaginary parts describe oscillation frequency. For the downward linear model with b>0, both poles lie in the left half-plane. Stability alone does not tell us whether the settling time or amount of oscillation is acceptable.

How does this connect to standard second-order models and transfer functions?

Retaining the input gives Iδ̈+bδ̇+mgℓδ=u near the downward equilibrium. Rotational inertia I, damping b and restoring coefficient mgℓ play the roles of mass, damping and spring stiffness in a translational model. Comparing with the standard second-order form gives:

ωn=√(g/ℓ),　ζ=b/(2Iωn)
With zero initial conditions: Δ(s)/U(s)=1/(Is2+bs+mgℓ)

Δ(s) and U(s) are the Laplace transforms of angular deviation and torque. The undamped natural frequency ωn is in rad/s; damping ratio ζ is dimensionless. Free release can produce motion when u=0, so retain initial-condition terms when finding the complete response. Quadrupling the length halves ωn and doubles the small-amplitude undamped period.

Where does a first-order approximation fit?

Two first-order state equations still describe a second-order pendulum. A genuine reduction needs another approximation. With sufficiently strong damping, after the fast transient has decayed, inertia may be negligible. Near the downward equilibrium, this gives bθ̇+mgℓθ≈u, with time constant τ=b/(mgℓ).

This slow-motion approximation omits the fast initial transient. It cannot independently satisfy arbitrary initial angle and angular velocity, or represent an underdamped oscillation. Every simplification needs a range of validity.

From analysis to control: can we change the motion?

A pivot motor could use measured θ and ω to apply torque. Near the downward equilibrium, choose u=−kpθ−kdω, with kp,kd>0. The approximate closed-loop equation is Iθ̈+(b+kd)θ̇+(mgℓ+kp)θ=0. Feedback adds effective damping and restoring action. A real actuator also has torque limits, delay and measurement error.

Stabilising the upright position requires a model and controller designed about that equilibrium. Later, frequency response, Nyquist analysis and stability margins help assess how much variation a design can tolerate.
